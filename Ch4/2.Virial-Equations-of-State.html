Volumetric Equations of State#

Generalized Form of the Volumetric Equation of State#

Cubic forms of volumetric equations of state played a central role in thermodynamics at the turn of the 19th century and continue to be used today to compute changes in thermal variables in terms of changes in volumetric variables. As discussed in the first half of your course, the first and second laws of thermodynamics allow us to compute how changes of state of liquids and vapors describe the flows of energy and mass in various unit operations. A necessary part of this problem‑solving is the ability to obtain high‑quality volumetric and thermal data for single‑component and multicomponent substances and mixtures of interest.

The starting point for computing thermal variables from volumetric variables is the volumetric equation of state, which relates \(P\), \(T\), and \(V\). The ideal gas law is one form of a cubic equation of state, and for the purposes of this lecture we will write it as:

\[ \frac{P\underline{V}}{RT} = 1 \]

For convenience, we define the compressibility factor \(Z = \frac{P\underline{V}}{RT}\) so that more generalized forms of equations of state may be written compactly. Using the compressibility, it is possible to define different orders of equations of state by expressing the EOS as a polynomial of the form:

\[ 0 = \sum_{i=0}^{n} a_i Z^i \]

where the integer \(n\) is the highest order of the polynomial expression.

First‑Order Equations of State#

The ideal gas law is an example of a first‑order equation of state because the highest power of the compressibility in the ideal gas law is \(n = 1\). All first‑order equations of state can be written as:

\[ \beta Z + \gamma = 0 \]

where \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) are parameters of the first‑order polynomial expression. For the ideal gas law, \(\beta = 1\) and \(\gamma = -1\).

An interactive example of a plot of the ideal gas law is given below. In this example, by changing the temperature, you will notice that the line shape does not change, but the curve shifts vertically up or down with increases or decreases in temperature. The ideal gas is a single‑phase fluid at all temperatures, and its behavior is defined over all conceivable phase space, but it is only accurate in the fully expanded limit, \(\lim_{P \to 0}\) or \(\lim_{V \to \infty}\).

%matplotlib widget

from ipywidgets import *
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

# Define constants
R = 8.314  # J/mol*K

# Typical molar volume ranges fall in this value of density
V = np.logspace(1, 5, 201)  # cm^3/mol
T = 298  # Kelvin

def P_IG(T, V):
    return R * T / V  # units returned in bar

fig = plt.figure()
ax = fig.add_subplot(1, 1, 1)  # create a subplot for plotting

line, = plt.plot(V, P_IG(T, V), '-r', label=r'$IG_{T=%3.1f K}$' % T)

plt.yscale('log')
plt.xscale('log')
plt.ylabel('P (bar)')
plt.xlabel(r'$V$ $(cm^3/mol)$')

# Replots based on slider value
def update(T=273):
    line.set_data(V, P_IG(T, V))
    line.set_label(r'$IG_{T=%3.1f K}$' % T)
    plt.xlim(min(V), max(V))
    plt.ylim(min(P_IG(T, V)), max(P_IG(T, V)))
    plt.legend()
    fig.canvas.draw_idle()

interact(update, T=(1, 1000.0, 1.0));

Second Order Equations of State#

The Virial Equation of State is an example of a second-order equation of state because the highest power of the compressibility is \(n=2\) in the general form. All second-order equations of state can be written as:

\[ \alpha Z^2 + \beta Z+\gamma=0 \]

where \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) are parameters of the 1st order polynomial expression. Traditionally, the virial equation of state is given as \(\alpha=1\) \(\beta=-\dfrac{aP}{(RT)^2}\) and \(\gamma=0\)

The Virial equation of state is often not written in this explicit polynomial form, but instead in a form the reflects the expected deviation from the ideal gas law.

\[\dfrac{P\underline{V}}{RT}=1-\dfrac{a(T)}{RT\underline{V}}\]

or

\[Z=1-\dfrac{B(T)}{\underline{V}}\]
%matplotlib widget
from ipywidgets import *
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

R = 8.314  # J/mol*K

Cubic (Third Order) Equations of State#

Cubic equations of state are third‑order equations of state because the highest power of the compressibility is \(n = 3\) in the general form. All cubic equations of state can be written as:

\[ Z^3 + \alpha Z^2 + \beta Z + \gamma = 0 \]

where \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(\gamma\) are parameters of the third‑order polynomial expression. Cubic equations of state have a special place in the history of thermodynamics because Van der Waals’ equation of state was the first to provide quantitative predictions of vapor–liquid equilibrium. Van der Waals showed that only when the cubic term is included can equilibrium between a vapor and a liquid be calculated.

Van der Waals Equation of State — 1873#

Van der Waals’ equation of state has the form:

\[ P = \frac{RT}{\underline{V} - b} - \frac{a}{\underline{V}^2} \]

The Van der Waals equation has two parameters, \(a\) and \(b\).

  • \(a\) is the attraction parameter, representing favorable interactions between molecules at higher densities.

  • \(b\) is the excluded‑volume (repulsive) parameter, representing the effective molecular volume.

A notable feature of the Van der Waals EOS is its divergence in the limit:

\[ \lim_{\underline{V} \to b} P \to \infty \]

Redlich–Kwong (RK) — 1949#

  • EOS form:

\[ P = \frac{RT}{\underline{V} - b} - \frac{a(T)}{\sqrt{T}\,\underline{V}(\underline{V} + b)} \]

  • Temperature dependence:

\[ a(T) = \frac{a_0}{\sqrt{T}} \]

with \(a_0\) a constant derived from critical properties (often scaling as \(T_c^{2.5}/P_c\)).

The RK EOS improved upon Van der Waals but still performed poorly for liquid‑phase densities.

Soave–Redlich–Kwong (SRK) — 1972#

  • EOS form:

\[ P = \frac{RT}{\underline{V} - b} - \frac{a(T)}{\underline{V}(\underline{V} + b)} \]

  • Split into critical constant and temperature factor:

\[ a(T) = a_c\,\alpha(T) \]
\[ a_c = 0.42747\,\frac{R^2 T_c^2}{P_c}, \qquad b = 0.08664\,\frac{RT_c}{P_c} \]

  • Soave \(\alpha(T)\) function:

\[ \alpha(T) = \left[1 + \kappa\left(1 - \sqrt{T_r}\right)\right]^2, \qquad T_r = \frac{T}{T_c} \]
\[ \kappa = 0.480 + 1.574\,\omega - 0.176\,\omega^2 \]

Peng–Robinson (PR) — 1976#

  • EOS form:

\[ P = \frac{RT}{\underline{V} - b} - \frac{a(T)}{\underline{V}^2 + 2b\underline{V} - b^2} \]

where

\[ \alpha(T) = \left[1 + \kappa\left(1 - T_r^{1/2}\right)\right]^2, \qquad \kappa = 0.37464 + 1.54226\,\omega - 0.26992\,\omega^2 \]

Here, \(\omega\) is the acentric factor, and \(T_r\) is the reduced temperature.

  • Critical‑property form:

\[ a(T) = a_c\,\alpha(T) \]
\[ a_c = 0.45724\,\frac{R^2 T_c^2}{P_c}, \qquad b = 0.07780\,\frac{RT_c}{P_c} \]

The introduction of the acentric factor greatly improved accuracy for both pure components and mixtures.

%matplotlib widget
from ipywidgets import *
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

# Define constants
R = 8.314  # J/mol*K

# Temperature and EOS parameters
T = 298  # Kelvin
a = 0.2  # Pa*m^6/mol
b = 18   # cm^3/mol (excluded volume)

# Molar volume range (starts just above b)
V = np.logspace(np.log10(b), 5, 201)  # cm^3/mol

def P_IG(T, V):
    return R * T / V  # bar (scaled)

def P_VEOS(T, V, a):
    return R * T / V - a * 1e6 / V**2  # bar

def P_VDW(T, V, a, b):
    return R * T / (V - b) - a * 1e6 / V**2  # bar

fig = plt.figure()
ax = fig.add_subplot(1, 1, 1)

line1, = plt.plot(V, P_IG(T, V), '-r', label=r'$IG_{T=%3.1f K}$' % T)
line2, = plt.plot(V, P_VEOS(T, V, a), '-b', label=r'$EOS_{T=%3.1f K}$' % T)
line3, = plt.plot(V, P_VDW(T, V, a, b), '-g', label=r'$VDW_{T=%3.1f K}$' % T)

plt.yscale('log')
plt.xscale('log')
plt.ylabel('P (bar)')
plt.xlabel(r'$V$ (cm$^3$/mol)')
plt.legend()

def update(T=273, a=0.2, b=20):
    # Update curves
    line1.set_data(V, P_IG(T, V))
    line1.set_label(r'$IG_{T=%3.1f K}$' % T)

    line2.set_data(V, P_VEOS(T, V, a))
    line2.set_label(r'$EOS_{T=%3.1f K}$' % T)

    line3.set_data(V, P_VDW(T, V, a, b))
    line3.set_label(r'$VDW_{T=%3.1f K}$' % T)

    # Update axes
    plt.xlim(1.1 * b, max(V))
    plt.ylim(min(P_IG(T, V)), max(P_IG(T, V)) * 10)
    plt.legend()
    fig.canvas.draw_idle()

interact(update, T=(1, 1000.0, 1.0), a=(0.001, 1.0, 0.001), b=(1, 100, 1));

Use virial form:

\[ P \approx \frac{RT}{\underline{V}} - \frac{a}{\underline{V}^2} \]

Fit:

\[ \dfrac{P\underline{V}}{RT} = 1 - \frac{a}{RT \underline{V}} \]

You will note above that while the isotherm is linear int he large pressure limit, the intercept is not identical to 1. You’re seeing something completely real and completely expected — and it’s a beautiful little thermodynamic subtlety. The short version is:

Your plot should be linear at high pressure, but the intercept will not be exactly 1 unless you are truly in the asymptotic limit where both \(P \to \infty\) and \(V \to b\).

Why the intercept is not exactly 1#

You are using the Van der Waals EOS:

\[ P = \frac{RT}{\underline{V} - b} - \frac{a}{\underline{V}^2} \]

Multiply both sides by \(\underline{V}/(RT)\):

\[ \frac{P\underline{V}}{RT} = \frac{\underline{V}}{\underline{V}-b} - \frac{a}{RT\underline{V}} \]

Now consider the large‑density / large‑pressure limit, which means:

  • \(\underline{V} \to b^+\)

  • \(P \to \infty\)

  • the attractive term \(a/V^2\) becomes negligible

  • Theoretical asymptotic intercept: \(1\)

  • Next‑order corrected behavior: $\( \frac{PV}{RT} \approx 1 + \frac{Pb}{RT} - \frac{a}{b^2 P} \)$

which explains why the fitted line in the “large pressure” region doesn’t quite hit 1 on the y‑axis.

  1. Generate a plot of the Peng-Robinson Equation of State for methane. Please provide at least 5 isotherms spanning a range of reduced temperatures including 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0, 2.0.

  • Describe the general featuers of the isotherms above and below the critical temperature.

  • As the molecular weight of the hydrocarbon increases, it has an interesting effect on the the parameters \(a\) and \(b\). Based on these changes, wht do you observe about changes in \(a\) and \(b\).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Peng–Robinson EOS parameters for methane
R = 8.31446261815324          # J/(mol·K)
Tc = 190.56                   # K
Pc = 4.5992e6                 # Pa
omega = 0.011                 # acentric factor (methane)

# PR EOS constants
kappa = 0.37464 + 1.54226*omega - 0.26992*omega**2
a = 0.45724 * R**2 * Tc**2 / Pc
b = 0.07780 * R * Tc / Pc

# Reduced temperatures to plot
Tr_list = [0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0, 2.0]

# Molar \underline{V}olume range (a\underline{V}oid \underline{V} = b singularity)
V = np.linspace(1.01*b, 8.0 * R*Tc/Pc, 800)   # m^3/mol

plt.figure(figsize=(3, 3), dpi=300, constrained_layout=True)

for Tr in Tr_list:
    T = Tr * Tc
    alpha = (1 + kappa * (1 - np.sqrt(Tr)))**2
    P = (R*T)/(V - b) - (a*alpha)/(V**2 + 2*b*V - b**2)   # Pa

    # Reduced \underline{V}ariables
    Vr = Pc * V / (R * Tc)
    Pr = P / Pc

    # Filter out any negati\underline{V}e or non-physical pressures for plotting
    mask = Pr > 0
    plt.plot(Vr, Pr, label=f"$T_r$ = {Tr:g}")

plt.xlabel(r"Reduced molar Volume $V_r = \dfrac{P_c V}{R T_c}$")
plt.ylabel(r"Reduced pressure $P_r = \dfrac{P}{P_c}$")

plt.legend()
plt.yscale('log')
plt.xscale('log')
plt.show()
